Debt, assets, power, and exit

Simon Dixon’s debt-power map

A beginner-friendly map of the worldview in the interview: modern politics sits underneath capital allocation, and the real leverage is debt, cheap money, assets, institutions, and attention.

Peter McCormack with Simon Dixon · Simon Dixon episode notes

Simon Dixon’s core claim: debt becomes power

Debt-to-power loop in plain English Modern money is largely created through debt. Debt creates dependence. The people closest to cheap money buy assets. Assets create wealth and influence. Governments borrow in the public’s name and absorb the blame. Crises, wars, bailouts, and reconstruction projects speed up the transfer from wage-earners to asset-owners. The practical escape is to reduce dependence: less bad debt, more resilient assets, self-custody where possible, local spending, family, and community.

Do not read this as a normal left-versus-right political argument. Read it as a leverage map: before a politician speaks, someone already controls credit, asset prices, refinancing, lobbying, contracts, media incentives, and institutional survival.

Memory sentence: Debt creates dependence; cheap capital buys assets; assets create power; power shapes policy; policy creates more debt.

The six-stage debt-to-power loop

The useful mental move is to stop beginning with politicians and start beginning with balance sheets.

1
Credit expands.Banks, governments, and markets create new claims through loans, deficits, bonds, and refinancing.
2
Dependence grows.Households need mortgages. Firms need financing. States need bond buyers. Everyone needs rollover.
3
Cheap capital buys assets.The best-connected institutions can buy companies, property, infrastructure, resources, bonds, and equities.
4
Assets become leverage.Ownership gives cash flow, collateral, board influence, voting rights, lobbying power, and media reach.
5
Policy follows leverage.Government spending, bailout design, military budgets, regulation, and crisis management start to favor the capital network.
6
The public pays.Inflation, taxes, wage pressure, rent, debt service, insecurity, and lower opportunity land on ordinary people.
Keep this: the interview is about leverage before voting. Elections happen inside a financial architecture that already rewards some players and weakens others.

Five key terms: debt, capital, assets, government, exit

Learn these first. They are the handles for the rest of the page.

1 · Debt

Debt as a future obligation

Debt is not only money owed. It is a future obligation. If you must repay, refinance, or roll over, someone else has leverage over your options.

2 · Capital

Capital as buying power

Capital means money that can be deployed. Cheap capital is a superpower because it lets institutions buy assets before everyone else can.

3 · Assets

Assets as inflation shields

Assets are property, stocks, companies, bonds, land, gold, Bitcoin, infrastructure, and productive businesses. They can rise while wages lag.

4 · State

State as borrower and blame sponge

The state borrows, spends, regulates, taxes, bails out, and fights wars. It is visible, so it absorbs public anger.

5 · Exit

Exit as fewer weak points

Exit means fewer weak points: less bad debt, more useful assets, stronger custody, local supply chains, better health, and real relationships.

Clear-head rule

Evidence rule: profit is not proof of planning

If a weapons company profits from a war, that proves an incentive. It does not by itself prove the company caused the war. Follow the money, but do not skip the evidence step.

The £100 loan that explains the system

Start with one simple loan. The Bank of England explains that most modern money is created when commercial banks make loans. Simon uses this mechanism as the seed of the whole power map.

Keep this: debt is not just a number. It is a behavioral leash. At personal, corporate, and national scale, refinancing becomes power.

Asset managers

Corporations

Military-industrial complex

Turns conflict, fear, and procurement budgets into revenue for defense and security firms.

Technical-industrial complex

Turns data, identity, cloud, AI, algorithms, digital money, and surveillance tools into infrastructure.

Media and algorithms

Sovereign wealth funds

State investment pools, often linked to energy or resource wealth, that partner with global capital.

Ordinary people

Workers, taxpayers, renters, mortgage holders, consumers, voters, savers, and data sources.

How the debt-power system works step by step

Read this as a sequence, not a list.

Act 1

Money starts as debt

When money is created through lending, the economy needs continuous income, borrowing, and refinancing. That makes society sensitive to interest rates, bank decisions, credit markets, and bond markets.

Act 2

Cheap capital buys the world

The player borrowing at 2% can outbid the player borrowing at 20%. Over time, cheap capital collects assets while expensive capital creates dependency.

Act 3

Government becomes the piggy bank

When crisis hits, government borrows in the public’s name. The spending often lands in large institutions: banks, defense firms, infrastructure firms, tech firms, insurers, and major contractors.

Act 4

Asset owners pull away

QE, bailouts, low rates, subsidies, and fiscal spending can raise asset prices. People with assets gain; people living only on wages and debt face rising costs.

Asset managers as the capital nervous system

Asset managers matter because they sit where pensions, ETFs, insurance money, public companies, proxy voting, and sovereign capital meet. BlackRock, Vanguard, and State Street are not simply rich people with vaults of cash; they manage other people’s capital at gigantic scale. That makes them central routers of financial influence.

Pensions and ETFs→asset managers→shares and votes→boards and policy pressure→capital allocation

Research on the “Big Three” asset managers found that BlackRock, Vanguard, and State Street together were the largest shareholder in most S&P 500 firms. BlackRock also offers some clients voting choice, which means the influence is large but not as simple as one person personally voting every share.

Industrial complexes: finance, war, and data

Finance

Financial-industrial complex

Banks, asset managers, private equity, insurers, bond markets, hedge funds, and investment banks. It turns debt, savings, pensions, and crises into asset control.

War

Military-industrial complex

Defense contractors, security agencies, military budgets, NATO procurement, intelligence-linked contracting, and reconstruction after conflict.

Data

Technical-industrial complex

Big tech, cloud, AI, data centers, digital ID, surveillance, cybersecurity, stablecoins, and central-bank digital currency infrastructure.

Keep this: crisis is not only a tragedy. It is also a budgeting event, a contracting event, a media event, and an asset-pricing event.

Countries as portfolios

The most unsettling part of the worldview is the idea that capital treats countries less like communities and more like asset bundles.

Country as home

A country is people, language, food, homes, grandparents, schools, land, culture, memories, and belonging.

Country as asset portfolio

A country is oil, ports, labor, debt, minerals, real estate, tax capacity, farmland, water, energy, and privatization opportunities.

Interview examples: empire, war, media, and Bitcoin

Once the loop is clear, the examples become easier to follow. Each example asks the same questions: where is the debt, who gets cheap capital, which assets move, who gets contracts, and what story is sold to the public?

Origin story

Bank of England and war finance

The starting image is the state needing money for war, finance providing it, and public debt becoming a permanent structure of power.

International debt

IMF loans and privatization

Debt trouble becomes leverage. Loans, conditions, reforms, privatization, and resource access can turn a country into a cash-flow portfolio.

War spending

Ukraine and reconstruction

The war is read through weapons contracts, European dependency, government-backed financing, and future reconstruction assets.

Resource politics

Venezuela, oil, and bargaining power

Resource-rich countries are understood through oil access, sanctions, debt pressure, military pressure, and who controls the terms of trade.

Empire decline

The dollar and slow Western squeeze

The predicted collapse is not cinematic. It is smaller homes, higher costs, more debt, fewer children, longer work, and lower expectations.

New power centers

China, Gulf capital, Africa, and multipolarity

Countries that control resources, manufacturing, energy, or sovereign wealth can play a longer game than wage-tax-and-debt economies.

Attention layer

Media and algorithmic boxes

Feeds sort people into fear and identity camps. The more emotional the feed, the easier the public is to predict, divide, and redirect.

Exit layer

Bitcoin, gold, and local life as exits

The positive answer is not winning the empire game. It is opting out of as many dependency loops as possible.

Keep this: every example is a variation of the same template: debt pressure, capital access, asset transfer, public narrative, and personal exit.

Bitcoin and gold as financial exit tools

Bitcoin appears near the end because it is the proposed boycott tool. The point is not merely price. The point is custody.

Inside the system

Captured exposure: ETF, custodian, leverage

You own exposure, but someone else controls custody. If you borrow against it, you turn a long-term asset into a debt contract with deadlines, margin calls, and counterparty risk.

Outside the system

Exit exposure: self-custody, node, no leverage

You reduce dependence on banks, custodians, lenders, and asset managers. This is why Simon treats self-custody as a political and financial act.

Keep this: the asset is one issue; custody is the deeper issue. In this worldview, an asset held through the same institutions you are trying to escape is only a partial exit.

Practical resilience plan: debt, assets, local life

The answer is not perfection. It is becoming less dependent each month.

Kill the worst debt first.

High-interest consumer debt is the most obvious leash. Every repayment removes a point of control.

Build an emergency buffer.

Cash is not a perfect long-term asset, but it prevents forced selling and panic borrowing.

Own something scarce or productive.

This could be Bitcoin, gold, property without dangerous leverage, a business, tools, land, or skills that generate income.

Avoid leverage traps.

Borrowing against volatile assets can convert freedom into a margin-call machine.

Move spending closer to home.

Local farmers, butchers, cafés, tradespeople, clubs, and independent businesses keep more energy in your real network.

Protect attention.

Algorithms can turn fear and outrage into a daily cage. Deliberate feeds, long walks, books, and real conversations are part of the exit.

Use time altruistically.

Simon’s positive ending is not “be selfish.” It is: look after yourself so you can help family, friends, and community see the game more clearly.

One-month target Owe less bad debt, own a little more of a resilient asset, spend a little more locally, and spend less time inside rage algorithms.

Two-minute teach-back script

Simon Dixon’s worldview says modern power is not mainly held by elected politicians. It is held by the capital-allocation network: banks, asset managers, corporations, sovereign wealth funds, military contractors, tech infrastructure, and the media systems that protect those flows.

Money enters through debt. Debt creates dependence. Cheap capital buys assets. Assets create power. Government borrows and spends in the public’s name. Crises accelerate the transfer. The answer is to reduce dependence: less bad debt, more resilient assets, self-custody where possible, local spending, attention control, family, and community.

Why this study method works

Dunloski et al. rate practice testing and distributed practice as high-utility learning techniques. The Learning Scientists also emphasize spacing and retrieval. For ADHD and dyslexia, the added trick is to make the information visible, chunked, spoken, and revisited in short sessions.

Closed-book checks

Try to answer before revealing. Imperfect answers are fine; retrieval is the point.

1. What is the central loop?
Debt creates dependence → cheap capital buys assets → assets create power → power shapes policy → policy creates more debt.
2. Why does debt matter so much?
Because anyone who must repay, refinance, or roll over debt is vulnerable to whoever controls credit, rates, liquidity, and market access.
3. Why are asset managers central?
They manage huge pools of pensions, ETFs, insurance money, and institutional capital. That gives them influence over ownership, voting, boards, and capital flows.
4. What is the K-shaped economy?
A split where asset owners move up while wage-only households move down because costs and asset prices rise faster than wages.
5. What is the difference between Bitcoin exposure and Bitcoin exit?
Exposure can be held through ETFs or custodians. Exit means self-custody, no leverage, and less dependence on the same financial institutions.
6. What is the practical answer?
Reduce dependence: kill bad debt, own useful assets, avoid leverage traps, self-custody where possible, buy local, protect attention, and build family/community resilience.

Memory card: debt machine, capital game, local exit

Debt machine, capital game, local exit The modern world is a debt machine. The winners are closest to cheap capital. The losers live mainly on wages and expensive debt. Politics manages the story. Finance manages the game. The escape is not a perfect revolution; it is a steady reduction of dependence at the personal, family, and community level.
